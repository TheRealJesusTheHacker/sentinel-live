## What happened

Ransomware has become an affiliate business: one crew builds
the tools, others rent them and run the break-ins. The newer
renters are using AI assistance to move faster — picking
targets, getting in, and locking systems at a pace a person
working by hand cannot match. The defense that decides whether
a bad night becomes a bad quarter has not changed, though. It
is whether you can put your data back.

## Why it matters here

Two failure stories repeat. In the first, the backup sat on the
same network as everything else, so when the attack came it was
encrypted right along with the files it was protecting — a
backup the attacker can reach is just a second copy of the
problem. In the second, the backup ran faithfully every night
and had never once been restored, so nobody knew it was
capturing the wrong folder, or damaged files, until the one day
it was needed. Both businesses believed they had backups. What
they had was a hope with a schedule.

## What to do — the three-point check

- **One copy the network cannot reach.** Keep at least one
  backup offline or off-site — an external drive that gets
  unplugged, or a cloud copy an intruder on your network cannot
  touch.
- **One restore you have actually done.** Pick a real file,
  restore it from the backup, and open it. That ten minutes is
  the difference between a backup and a belief.
- **One calendar entry to do it again.** A backup decays as
  your systems change. Test the restore a few times a year, the
  way you would test a smoke alarm.

**This month's version:** restore one file from your backup
and open it. If it works, you have a backup. If it does not,
you found out on a calm day — the only good day to find out.

## The practice round

The [phishing training page](/training/phishing) shows six
messages drawn the way the real ones look: an account
warning, a delivery notice, a receipt, a favor from the
boss. For each one you make the call — legit or phish —
and the page explains why. Nothing is clickable and
nothing sends anywhere; the samples are mock-ups built for
training. The point is not memorizing six messages. It is
learning the six tells they run on, because the real ones
run on the same six.

## The six tells

- **The sender's real address.** A message can call itself
  anything; the display name is a costume. Read the address
  behind it — on a phone, tap the sender to see it in full.
  One swapped letter in a familiar domain, a zero standing
  in for an o, is the whole trick.
- **The link's real destination.** Hover on a computer, or
  press and hold on a phone, and read where a link actually
  goes before you land there. A familiar name with one
  wrong letter in it is not the familiar name.
- **The hurry.** "Locked in one hour." "Final notice."
  "Act now." Urgency is a tool, built to stop you thinking.
  Real account problems survive the two minutes it takes
  to check through the front door.
- **The surprise.** An invoice you never ordered, a
  delivery you never scheduled, a form you never asked
  for. Unexpected is a tell all by itself. Verify with the
  sender on a number you already have — never a number the
  message hands you.
- **The keys, asked for outright.** A message that wants
  your password and your sign-in code together is asking
  for the account itself. The code is the second lock; no
  real sign-in you reach from an email asks you to hand
  both over.
- **The process that breaks pattern.** Gift cards bought
  in secret for a manager stuck in a meeting. Bank details
  returned as an email form against a payday deadline. When
  a request routes around the way things are normally done
  — and around anyone who could confirm it — the request
  itself is the tell.

## The habit that defeats most of them

Under all six sits one habit: **go around.** Open the app
or type the address yourself instead of using the link you
were handed. Messages that respect that rule — no link at
all, "sign in the way you normally do" — are what the
genuine ones look like, and the two real messages in the
round read exactly that way. Make "stop and ask" a house
rule, too: a ten-second call settles what secrecy is
designed to rush past, and nobody should ever be in
trouble for making it.

**The ten-minute version:** take the [practice
round](/training/phishing) with everyone who opens email
or pays bills, and put the tells on the wall where the
inbox gets worked. If a message like these is already
sitting in a real inbox and you want it looked at before
anyone touches it, start at [the contact
page](/contact).

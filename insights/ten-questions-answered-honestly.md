## What the questions are really asking

The ten questions on the [self-assessment](/assessment) are
not a quiz about technology. Every one of them is asking
about a door: who can open it, whether it is locked, and
whether you would know if somebody walked through it. Read
that way, the questions group themselves.

**The front door.** The router question asks whether the
password on the box that guards your network is still the
one printed in its manual — a password anyone can look up.
The Wi-Fi question asks whether guests and personal phones
share a network with the machines that hold your records.
The locks question asks whether screens lock themselves and
the network gear sits behind a door that stays shut.

**Whether the house repairs itself.** The updates question
is about memory: systems that patch themselves do not
depend on somebody remembering patch day, and the window
between a flaw going public and attacks using it is now
measured in days, sometimes hours. The antivirus question
is about coverage — protection on most machines is not
protection, and the machine it misses is usually the one
in the back office nobody thinks about.

**Whether you can get back up.** The backups question is
two questions wearing one coat: do copies exist somewhere
an attacker inside your network cannot reach, and have you
ever actually restored from them. The plan question asks
whether, at 9 a.m. on a bad morning, everyone knows the
first call to make, what to unplug, and what not to touch.

**Whether people are the lock or the gap.** The password
and sign-in questions ask whether one stolen password
opens one account or every account — and whether a second
step stands behind the important ones. The training
question asks whether the people who open email and pay
bills can spot a fake, and whether it is safe for them to
stop and ask when something looks wrong.

## What to do about a weak answer

- **Fix the widest gap first.** The results page orders
  your checklist that way on purpose — widest opening at
  the top. Start with the gap an attacker would use, not
  the one that is easiest to close.
- **Treat "not sure" as a "no" with homework.** It scores
  the same as "no," because an attacker will not grade on
  a curve. Finding out is usually a ten-minute look.
- **One finished fix beats ten intentions.** Change the
  router password tonight. Turn on automatic updates and
  multi-factor authentication this week. A short list
  finished beats a long list framed.
- **Write down where things are kept.** A changed password
  nobody can find is its own small emergency. Note where
  the new one lives — in a password manager, ideally — and
  who can get to it.

Breach Check

Two checks, run the careful way. The first looks up whether an email address appears in known public breaches. The second checks whether a password has shown up in breach data — without the password itself ever leaving your browser. This is Sentinel Safeguard's own page, built on public breach data; it is not the Have I Been Pwned website, though the password check uses that service's Pwned Passwords data in the privacy-preserving way described below.

Email Breach Lookup Password Exposure Check What To Do Now How It Works & Privacy

Tool one

Email breach lookup

Enter an email address and see which known public breaches it appears in, when they happened, and what kind of data they exposed. Breach data comes from XposedOrNot, a public breach-data project — to run the lookup, the address you enter is sent to XposedOrNot by Sentinel's server. That is stated plainly because it is how the check works; what Sentinel itself keeps is covered below (nothing).

Tool two

Password exposure check

Type a password and find out whether it has appeared in known breach data, and roughly how many times. The method is called k-anonymity: your browser turns the password into a SHA-1 fingerprint, and only the first 5 characters of that fingerprint are sent to Sentinel's server, which asks Have I Been Pwned's Pwned Passwords service for every fingerprint starting with those 5 characters. Your browser then finds its own fingerprint in that list, on your device.

Your full password and its full hash are never transmitted or stored. They never leave your browser in any form. The only thing that travels is the 5-character prefix — a fragment shared by thousands of different passwords.

What to do now

  • Change that password everywhere it is reused. A breached password is tried against other sites automatically — the same password anywhere else is the real danger, not the old breach itself.
  • Turn on multi-factor authentication (MFA) on the account, and on your email account first of all — whoever holds your email can reset almost everything else.
  • Use a unique password for every account, kept in a password manager, so one breach can only ever cost you one account.
  • Consider a credit freeze with the credit bureaus if the breach exposed identity data — a Social Security number, date of birth, or financial account numbers. A freeze is free and blocks new accounts opened in your name.
  • Watch the accounts the breach touched for password-reset mail and sign-in alerts you did not start.

Method, sources, and privacy

How it works — and what is sent where

Email lookups are powered by XposedOrNot. When you run one, Sentinel's server sends the email address you entered to XposedOrNot's public API, reads back the names of the breaches that address appears in, and joins them against XposedOrNot's breach metadata (breach date, the site involved, and the kinds of data exposed) so the results above can show real detail instead of bare names. Source for both the match and the breach details: XposedOrNot.

Password checks use Have I Been Pwned's Pwned Passwords range service. Only the first 5 characters of your password's SHA-1 fingerprint reach Sentinel's server, and only that prefix is passed on to Pwned Passwords. The answer is a list of fingerprint endings — your browser compares its own ending against the list locally, so the match itself happens on your device.

What Sentinel keeps: nothing. We never store a searched email address, a password, a full hash, or a hash prefix — not in a database, not in a file, not in a log. The checks run in the moment and are gone when the answer is shown. Like every public page here, this page view itself is counted in the site's visitor log (the page, never what you typed) — see the privacy page for exactly what that log holds. A clean result means the address or password was not found in the data these services hold; it is not a guarantee that it has never been exposed anywhere.
